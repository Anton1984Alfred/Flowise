## 1. Matrix differences: whole blood (DBS) vs. plasma/serum (phospholipids) vs. erythrocytes (RBC): absolute values, time integration, correlations, acute meal effect

### Takeaway
RBC EPA+DHA (the Omega-3 Index) integrates intake over about 2–4 months, has the lowest within-person variability (CV about 4%) and does not change after an acute fish-oil dose. Plasma reacts within hours (+47% at 6 h). Whole blood (DBS) mixes cells and plasma, so it sits in between: it correlates strongly with RBC (r² ≈ 0.79; r ≈ 0.88) but gives systematically *different absolute numbers*, usually lower EPA+DHA % and a higher n-6:n-3 ratio. A whole-blood % value is therefore not an Omega-3 Index unless it is converted.

### Cited Findings
**Absolute-value differences**
- Whole blood is "a mixture of both cells and plasma. Since these portions have different omega-3 levels, you're not going to get the same number from a spot of whole blood vs. just the red blood cells". OmegaQuant (Harris) therefore converts DBS EPA+DHA to an RBC-equivalent value with an equation (company FAQ). — [OmegaQuant FAQ](https://omegaquant.com/frequent-question/why-dont-the-epa-and-dha-values-add-up-to-the-omega-3-index-on-my-dried-blood-spot-dbs-report/)
- Bell JG et al. 2011 (Br J Nutr 106:1408–15; n = 51 UK patients, 27 without supplements; fingertip whole blood vs. venous RBC polar lipids): EPA, DHA, AA, AA:EPA and %n-3 HUFA in total HUFA correlated well between the fractions. However, **all 35 fatty-acid values differed significantly between RBC-PL and whole blood except 20:2n-6 and %n-3 HUFA in HUFA**, and **the n-6:n-3 ratio was higher in whole blood**. — [Bell et al. 2011, Br J Nutr](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/using-a-fingertip-whole-blood-sample-for-rapid-fatty-acid-measurement-method-validation-and-correlation-with-erythrocyte-polar-lipid-compositions-in-uk-subjects/016380E588A2B7FF348283D63AC1054F)
- An abstract attributed to Bell's DBS work reports capillary whole blood vs. venous RBC/PL LC-PUFA at R² 0.64–0.86, but only R² 0.31–0.41 for arachidonic acid. Whether this belongs to the 2011 paper is unconfirmed (verify). — [Bell et al. 2011 (search excerpt)](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/using-a-fingertip-whole-blood-sample-for-rapid-fatty-acid-measurement-method-validation-and-correlation-with-erythrocyte-polar-lipid-compositions-in-uk-subjects/016380E588A2B7FF348283D63AC1054F)
- A company podcast (Harris, OmegaQuant) says it is "extremely difficult to get a whole blood EPA+DHA over 8%", and that the RBC-derived targets "should not be used for whole blood tests from any lab". It gives an informal equivalence of DBS 5.4% ≈ RBC 8% (company statement, not peer-reviewed). — [OmegaMatters Ep. 48](https://omegaquant.com/omegamatters-ep-48/)
- The OmegaQuant FAQ shows that plasma and RBC values differ even within one lab ("numbers will be quite different, even from the same lab when comparing red blood cells with plasma"). — [OmegaQuant FAQ](https://omegaquant.com/frequent-question/how-is-the-omega-3-index-different-from-other-fatty-acid-profile-tests/)

**Correlations between matrices**
- Stark KD, Aristizabal Henao JJ, Metherel AH, Pilote L. PLEFA 2016;104:1–10 (n = 1,104 acute coronary syndrome patients, GENESIS-PRAXY): RBC EPA+DHA correlated with **whole blood r² = 0.79** and with **plasma r² = 0.54** (both p < 0.001). Multiple regression to predict RBC EPA+DHA reached **R² = 0.84 (whole blood)** and **R² = 0.70 (plasma)**. The authors note that estimating RBC from plasma "appears to require translation by categorization rather than … as continuous data". — [Stark et al. 2016, PLEFA](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0952327815300235)
- Sparkes C et al. 2020 (Nutrients 12:1017; dose-response trial, 0.35 g/d and 1.0 g/d EPA+DHA; n = 45 with complete data): at baseline, RBC EPA+DHA correlated with plasma at **r = 0.87** and whole blood at **r = 0.88**. The *response* to supplementation varied strongly between people (response CV ≈ 0.32 in plasma and ≈ 0.29 in whole blood; RBC value truncated in the snippet). — [Sparkes et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7231102/)
- Meyer BJ et al. 2021 (Nutrients 13:1419; premenopausal women; 0, 0.35, 0.7, 1.05 g/d for 8 weeks; about 11–15 per arm): fingertip whole blood was "an effective alternative to erythrocytes and plasma" as a biomarker of n-3 LCPUFA intake. Correlation with intake was r = 0.38–0.46 for EPA and DHA. The authors caution that the fingertip method used "is known to result in poor recovery of EPA and DHA". The fingertip total n-3 range was 3.1–9.2 mol%. — [Meyer et al. 2021](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8147031/)
- Systematic review of diet vs. biomarker: correlation of intake with RBC EPA r = 0.23–0.38 and with RBC DHA r = 0.19–0.56. — [Br J Nutr systematic review](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/dietary-methods-and-biomarkers-of-omega-3-fatty-acids-a-systematic-review/39035617BC48B516AF1A95CC49525F53)
- Fingertip blood on filter paper (direct methylation) reflected venous whole-blood fatty acids. Of 8 fatty acids, capillary DHA showed the strongest correlation with venous DHA (PLEFA 2010). — [PubMed 21093236](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21093236/)
- Stark et al. 2016 global survey (Prog Lipid Res): of the pooled studies, 33% used plasma total lipids, 32% plasma PL, 32% RBC and **only 3% whole blood**. All values were converted to "RBC-equivalents" for mapping. — [PubMed 27216485](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27216485/)

**Time integration and acute effects**
- Harris WS, Varvel SA, Pottala JV, Warnick GR, McConnell JP. J Clin Lipidol 2013;7:433–40: after one fish-oil dose, **plasma EPA+DHA rose 47% at 6 h**, while **RBC EPA+DHA did not change**. At 24 h the plasma change was about **10-fold larger** than the RBC change. Conclusion: an acute dose within a day before testing raises plasma, and RBC are the more reliable chronic marker (an HbA1c analogy). The study was funded and promoted by Health Diagnostic Laboratory, which had an interest in RBC testing. — [J Clin Lipidol 2013](https://www.lipidjournal.com/article/S1933-2874(13)00198-0/fulltext); [BioSpace](https://www.biospace.com/health-diagnostic-laboratory-inc-researchers-compare-effects-of-acute-fish-oil-dose-on-plasma-vs-red-blood-cell-fatty-acid-levels)
- Harris WS & Thomas RM, Clin Biochem 2010;43:338–40 (n = 20, weekly sampling for 6 weeks): within-subject CV was **4.1 ± 1.9% for RBC EPA+DHA**, **15.9 ± 6.4% for plasma** and **14.5 ± 8.4% for plasma phospholipids**. RBC omega-3 content "was not altered in the fed state". — [Harris & Thomas 2010 (OHSU record)](https://ohsu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/biological-variability-of-blood-omega-3-biomarkers-2/); [OmegaQuant methodological document](https://omegaquant.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/09/omegaquant_methodological.pdf)
- Katan MB et al. 1997 (J Lipid Res; 18-month controlled study): EPA incorporation into RBC membranes had a **half-life of about 28 days**, and **steady state was reached after about 180 days**. DHA changes were "erratic and smaller", so no firm DHA half-life could be given. — [PubMed 9374124](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9374124/)
- In 60 placebo-treated patients on a stable diet, the mean HS-Omega-3 Index did not change over 10 weeks (company methods document). — [OmegaQuant methodological document](https://omegaquant.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/09/omegaquant_methodological.pdf)
- von Schacky's 2024/2025 editorial (J Clin Lipidol 2025;19:208–214): biological variability is high in plasma and plasma PL and low in RBC. A search summary gives whole-blood variability as roughly 6–7% (verify this figure in the full text). — [von Schacky, J Clin Lipidol](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1933287424002708)

### Inferences
- A whole-blood/DBS EPA+DHA % is a hybrid marker. Its plasma share responds within hours to a fish meal or capsule, so a DBS value is more sensitive to recent intake than an RBC value, though less sensitive than plasma. No study was found that quantifies the post-meal rise in DBS specifically (see Gaps). Practical consequence: a DBS test taken shortly after a capsule or fish meal can overstate chronic status somewhat, while an RBC test cannot.
- Whole-blood fatty-acid percentages, and ratios derived from them such as n-6:n-3 and AA:EPA, are systematically different from RBC values (Bell 2011). Cut-offs derived in RBC, such as the Omega-3 Index 8% target or the <4% risk zone, cannot be applied to raw whole-blood numbers.
- The time-integration claims for whole blood ("reflects the last 120 days") are only partly justified: the RBC fraction integrates over weeks to months, but the plasma fraction does not.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed study was found that directly quantifies how much a single fish meal or capsule raises DBS whole-blood EPA+DHA after 6–24 h. A Waterloo thesis, "Omega-3 Fatty Acid Blood Biomarkers Before and After Acute Fish Oil", may contain this but could not be read. — [UWSpace](https://uwspace.uwaterloo.ca/bitstreams/7e0b8515-6c05-4ee7-becb-d13b36c6884f/download)
- The exact R² values of Meyer 2021 (fingertip WB vs. RBC/plasma) and the RBC response CV of Sparkes 2020 were truncated in the snippets.

## 2. Conversion equations from whole-blood/DBS EPA+DHA to an RBC-equivalent Omega-3 Index

### Takeaway
Conversion equations exist (Harris/OmegaQuant DBS method 2016; Stark et al. 2016 for whole blood and plasma). At the *population* level they achieve r ≈ 0.96–0.98 or R² ≈ 0.84. However, the published coefficients are poorly accessible, each is specific to one lab method, and individual prediction error was not reported in an accessible form. A converted DBS value is an *estimate* of the Omega-3 Index, not a measurement of it. Which equation a consumer lab uses, if it uses one at all, is decisive.

### Cited Findings
- Harris WS & Polreis J. "Measurement of the Omega-3 Index in Dried Blood Spots", Ann Clin Lab Res 2016 (DOI 10.21767/2386-5180.1000137): derivation set of 49 and validation set of 49 samples. An equation built from RBC data predicted the observed Omega-3 Index with **r ≈ 0.96–0.98**. The snippet renders the fit as "Omega-3 index = 1.0 × DBS EPA+DHA − 0.000049, r = 0.96". This is very likely the *predicted vs. observed* line rather than the conversion equation itself (the snippet is garbled; verify). The ResearchGate version reports 98 samples vs. 49 in the journal copy. — [Harris & Polreis 2016 (PDF)](https://omegaquant.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/DBS-Method-paper-WSH-JP-2016-Ann-Clin-Lab-Res.pdf); [journal copy](https://www.itmedicalteam.pl/articles/measurement-of-the-omega3-index-in-dried-blood-spots-102118.html)
- Harris et al. 2023 (Vet Sci; dogs/cats DBS→O3I): the dog equation is y = 0.709x + 0.0002 (R² = 0.98). The *correction notice* states that **"a DBS EPA+DHA value of 4% would translate into an O3I of 5.9% for humans"**. This implies that the human equation maps DBS values to clearly *higher* RBC values. The human equation must not be used for animals, and the original Table 1 values for dogs were wrong (corrected). — [Correction, PMC10367180](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC10367180/); [original, PMC9863621](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9863621/)
- Informal company equivalence: DBS 5.4% ≈ RBC 8% (OmegaQuant podcast). This is consistent with 4% → 5.9% but not peer-reviewed. — [OmegaMatters Ep. 48](https://omegaquant.com/omegamatters-ep-48/)
- Stark et al. 2016 PLEFA (ACS, n = 1,104): RBC EPA+DHA predicted from whole-blood fatty acids by multiple regression **R² = 0.84** (simple r² = 0.79) and from plasma R² = 0.70. Plasma required categorical rather than continuous translation. The exact coefficients were not retrievable. — [Stark et al. 2016](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0952327815300235)
- Plasma → RBC-equivalent conversions cited in a later pregnancy paper (secondary, not checked against the original): plasma total lipid y = 1.13x + 2.11; plasma PL y = 0.93x + 0.75; plasma phosphatidylcholine y = 0.6663x + 3.233. — [Global comparison of erythrocyte EPA+DHA in pregnant women, PMC12975356](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12975356/)
- The 2025 global DBS paper (>500,000 whole-blood DBS samples; Lipids Health Dis 2025; data from the Vitas/Zinzino test system per the company press release) states that DBS required "a correction" to express an Omega-3 Index. Its continental whole-blood total n-6:n-3 ratios ranged from 6.2:1 to 8.8:1. — [Lipids Health Dis 2025](https://lipidworld.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12944-025-02676-6); [Zinzino press release](https://news.cision.com/zinzino/r/zinzino-ab--publ--new-study-unveils-global-snapshot-on-omega-3-deficiency---and-why-high-precision-t,c4219442)
- A UK commercial provider claims R² > 0.96 against the RBC Omega-3 Index (marketing; not peer-reviewed). — [omega3index.co.uk](https://www.omega3index.co.uk/pages/our-method)

### Inferences
- An r of 0.96 between DBS and RBC means about 92% shared variance. This is fine for ranking people but still leaves a meaningful *individual* prediction error. With a typical between-person SD of the Omega-3 Index of about 2.1 percentage points (Harris 2017, mean 6.1%, SD 2.1%), the residual SD is ≈ √(1−0.92) × 2.1 ≈ 0.6 percentage points, so the 95% prediction interval is about ±1.2 percentage points. *This is my own calculation from r and SD; no published individual prediction error or Bland-Altman limits were retrievable.* An estimated "Omega-3 Index 7.5%" from DBS is therefore compatible with a true RBC value of roughly 6.3–8.7%.
- Equations are lab- and method-specific: they depend on the denominator panel, the derivatization and the card type. Applying one lab's equation to another lab's DBS data adds unknown bias.
- When a consumer DBS test reports a "whole-blood omega-3 %" or an "Omega-3 Index" without naming the conversion equation and its validation (n, r/R², bias, limits of agreement), its value is not traceable to the RBC-based risk literature.

### Gaps
- The exact human DBS→O3I coefficients (Harris & Polreis 2016), Bland-Altman limits and individual prediction errors could not be read (the PDF was not reachable). The Stark 2016 whole-blood→RBC coefficients could not be retrieved either.
- No independent (non-company) validation of a DBS→RBC conversion with published individual-level error was found.

## 3. DBS-specific pre-analytical issues: oxidation, antioxidant cards (BHT, FAPS, PUFAcoat), room-temperature/shipping stability, heat/humidity, hematocrit, spot volume, contamination, capillary vs. venous

### Takeaway
On untreated filter paper, long-chain PUFA (EPA, DHA) oxidize within days at room temperature: omega-3 biomarkers changed significantly already by **day 3**. Antioxidant-treated cards (BHT, the proprietary FAPS cocktail, PUFAcoat) extend stability to about 2–8 weeks, or up to about 3 months by manufacturer claim. Even with BHT, EPA+DHA fell from 3.2 to 2.6 wt% (−19%) by day 28. Heat and humidity were not systematically studied. Spot volume appears not to matter for the Omega-3 Index. No fatty-acid-specific data on hematocrit effects or skin contamination were found.

### Cited Findings
**Oxidation and antioxidants**
- Metherel AH, Hogg RC, Buzikievich LM, Stark KD. Lipids Health Dis 2013;12:22: on DBS **without BHT**, PUFA and omega-3 biomarkers (%n-3 HUFA in total HUFA, EPA+DHA, n-6/n-3 ratio) were "dramatically reduced" and changed significantly (p < 0.05) **after only 3 days** of room-temperature storage. With BHT, the fall in EPA+DHA was delayed: baseline **3.2 ± 0.2 wt% → 2.6 ± 0.03 wt% at 28 days** (≈ −19% relative). The marker %n-3 HUFA in HUFA showed **no difference up to 28 days with 5.0 mg/mL BHT**. Conclusion: storage "in sealed containers with BHT may prevent PUFA degradation for up to 8 weeks". Humidity was not controlled. Light, heat and humidity were named as possible mechanisms. — [Metherel et al. 2013](https://lipidworld.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/1476-511X-12-22); [PubMed 23425563](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23425563/)
- Metherel et al. 2019 (Lipids 54:755–761) states that BHT-treated DBS "appear stable for up to 30 days at room temperature". Participating labs were told to keep samples at ambient temperature until 15 days after spotting. — [Metherel et al. 2019](https://aocs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/lipd.12203)
- Metherel & Stark 2016 review (PLEFA): HUFA losses in whole blood stored at −20 °C appear to be caused by hemolysis and iron-initiated peroxidation. These can be prevented by glycerol, iron chelation and/or dried blood spotting. — [Metherel & Stark 2016](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-stability-of-blood-fatty-acids-during-storage-A-Metherel-Stark/c518fef680e42f17313a17c5aa31fe6d8150e966)
- Metherel et al. 2013 (Lipids): EPA and DHA in liquid whole blood fall *faster at −20 °C* than at room temperature, 4 °C or −75 °C (counter-intuitive freezer effect). — [Lipids 2013](https://aocs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1007/s11745-013-3827-x)
- Liu G, Mühlhäusler BS, Gibson RA. PLEFA 2014;91:251–260: developed a DBS system (antioxidant-coated paper, later sold as "PUFAcoat") to protect n-3 LCPUFA, comparing Fluka vs. Whatman paper with and without antioxidant. — [Liu et al. 2014](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/268821985_A_method_for_long_term_stabilisation_of_long_chain_polyunsaturated_fatty_acids_in_dried_blood_spots_and_its_clinical_application)
- A later LC-MS/MS paper (PLEFA 2017) states that PUFAcoat paper ensures PUFA stability at room temperature "for periods up to 3 months". A 2019 study notes that the manufacturer asks for analysis within 4 weeks of collection. — [PLEFA 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0952327817301655)
- Harris & Polreis 2016 (OmegaQuant DBS with "FAPS", a proprietary multi-component antioxidant cocktail): acceptance criterion was within 15% of baseline. Reported results: Omega-3 Index stable for 4 weeks refrigerated (max loss 6%); up to 3 years at −20 °C (max loss 13% at 2.5 years); ≥4 years at −80 °C (max loss 5.6%). Room-temperature stability is described as more than 6 weeks. The company states the index was "quite stable, certainly over the first four or five days" (a typical mailing window). These are company data, and no independent head-to-head FAPS test was found. — [Harris & Polreis 2016](https://omegaquant.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/DBS-Method-paper-WSH-JP-2016-Ann-Clin-Lab-Res.pdf); [OmegaQuant blog](https://omegaquant.com/what-factors-influence-the-omega-3-index/)
- Molecules 2018 (DBS lipidomics): without antioxidant, the SFA/UFA ratio rose by **9.7% after 15 days**, attributed to oxidation of unsaturated fatty acids. The SFA/MUFA ratio decreased by up to −24% (condition unclear) and by only −9% in the best-protected condition. — [Molecules 2018](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules23020403)
- Rhinoceros DBS cards (PeerJ 2022): total PUFA 34.3% initially vs. 29.0% after room-temperature (23 °C) storage, about −15% relative (animal data). — [PeerJ 2022](https://peerj.com/articles/12896/)
- Dried umbilical cord blood on filter paper at −28 °C: LC-PUFA should be analysed within a week, with major losses afterwards (Lipids 2015). — [Lipids 2015](https://aocs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1007/s11745-015-4111-z)
- Fingertip blood on filter paper (PLEFA 2010): prolonged storage at 4 °C had a deleterious effect, especially on DHA. — [PubMed 21093236](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21093236/)
- Porcine DBS (PerkinElmer Spot Saver card, Ahlstrom 226 paper; profile run within 1 week): of 30 fatty acids and groups, only 4 (behenic acid, n-3 DPA, nervonic acid, adrenic acid) differed from frozen whole blood. A single spot was sufficient, with extra spots collected in case of poor quality or contamination. — [Wood et al. 2021, PMC8419488](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC8419488/)

**Hematocrit, volume, capillary vs. venous, contamination**
- JAHA 2025 review "Dried Blood Spots to Assess Cardiovascular-Kidney-Metabolic Health": hematocrit changes blood viscosity and spot spread, so its effect must be assessed **biomarker by biomarker**. Hematocrit effects were significant for malondialdehyde (normalization by hemoglobin needed) and moderate for TMAO. Sample homogeneity is a recognized limitation. Contamination and estimating the extracted volume are listed as general DBS issues. For the Omega-3 Index, a study reported the index was **unaffected by the volume of blood** spotted (volume range truncated in the snippet). — [JAHA 2025, PMC12132672](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12132672/)
- Capillary vs. venous: hematocrit, RBC count and hemoglobin are about 1–3% higher in capillary samples (general hematology literature, not fatty-acid specific). — [ResearchGate: blood counts venous/fingertip/arterial](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11794750_Comparison_of_blood_counts_in_venous_fingertip_and_arterial_blood_and_their_measurement_variation)

### Inferences
- Because the Omega-3 Index and whole-blood EPA+DHA are *percentages of total fatty acids*, hematocrit and spot volume largely cancel out of the ratio. This is consistent with the volume-independence finding. Hematocrit does, however, shift the RBC:plasma mix within whole blood, and therefore which compartment dominates the DBS value. People with low or high hematocrit (e.g., anemia, altitude, sex differences) could get slightly different whole-blood values at the same RBC status. This is unquantified (see Gaps).
- Oxidation preferentially destroys the most unsaturated fatty acids (DHA 22:6 > EPA 20:5 > AA 20:4 > LA 18:2). Any oxidative loss during shipping therefore biases DBS results toward a *lower* omega-3 % and a *higher* n-6:n-3 and AA:EPA ratio. That makes the person look worse at baseline, which matters for before/after marketing designs (see section 7).
- Minimum quality criteria for a DBS omega-3 test: an antioxidant-treated card (BHT, FAPS, PUFAcoat or similar), a documented stability study at room temperature *and* under heat stress, a defined maximum transit time (stability data support roughly ≤2–4 weeks at room temperature with antioxidant; a few days without), and lab-side rejection rules (spot size, saturation, contamination).

### Gaps
- No quantitative data were found on heat (>30 °C, e.g., summer mail) or high humidity for antioxidant-treated fatty-acid DBS cards.
- No fatty-acid-specific study of capillary vs. venous DBS bias, hematocrit effect, or contamination by hand lotions, skin lipids or alcohol swabs was found. The skin-surface-lipid contamination risk is plausible but unquantified in the retrieved literature.
- No peer-reviewed, independent stability validation of the FAPS cocktail was found; OmegaQuant data only.

## 4. Analytical issues: denominator (number and identity of fatty acids), GC-FID vs. GC-MS, peak identification, and evidence that labs report different values for the same sample

### Takeaway
The Omega-3 Index is a *relative* value (EPA+DHA as % of the fatty acids *the lab chooses to count*). Different panels (e.g., about 11 vs. 19 vs. 24–26 fatty acids), different response-factor corrections and different peak identification produce different percentages from the same sample. Historically, one sample sent to five labs gave Omega-3 Index results differing **by a factor of 3.5**. Standardizing the reported fatty-acid set reduced inter-lab CVs for EPA from 8.6% to 6.0% and for DHA from 10.5% to 7.2% (DBS ring trial 2019).

### Cited Findings
- von Schacky (Nutrients 2014;6:799–814 and later reviews): one sample sent to five laboratories gave Omega-3 Index results differing **by a factor of 3.5**, although the labs offered "the same Omega-3 Index test using the same analytical method". Comparing results between labs was therefore "impossible". This was the reason for the trademarked, standardized **HS-Omega-3 Index**. — [von Schacky 2014, Nutrients](https://www.mdpi.com/2072-6643/6/2/799); [von Schacky, Proc Nutr Soc 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-nutrition-society/article/omega3-index-in-201819/8576C6B755BA9697A62A981E905D5DD7)
- The HS-Omega-3 Index is defined as EPA+DHA as a % of **26 fatty acids** in erythrocytes ("strictly standardized method, assessing another 24 fatty acids … rigorous quality assurance"). — [von Schacky 2014, LaboratoriumsMedizin](https://doi.org/10.1515/labmed-2014-0007); [von Schacky J Clin Lipidol 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1933287424002708)
- Metherel AH et al. 2019, "Interlaboratory Assessment of Dried Blood Spot Fatty Acid Compositions", Lipids 54:755–761: whole blood from 2 subjects (low and high n-3), 25 µL on BHT-treated strips, sent to **5 laboratories**. Results were reported as each lab's routine panel *and* as a standardized set of **19 fatty acids**. Outliers: 5 of 91 measures (routine) vs. 8 of 91 (standardized). Inter-lab **CV for EPA fell from 8.6% → 6.0% (low-n-3 subject) and 9.1% → 6.6% (high)**, and **for DHA from 10.5% → 7.2% and 10.5% → 6.6%**. — [Metherel et al. 2019](https://aocs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/lipd.12203)
- J Nutr 2024 narrative review of commercially available omega-3 tests: when the denominator was standardized, the reported fatty-acid percentages were "actually quite similar", which may explain part of the inter-lab variation. The review also criticizes that **some labs adopt target ranges from studies whose samples were analysed by a different lab using a different method**. — [J Nutr 2024](https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(24)00164-0/fulltext)
- OmegaQuant (company) states that unlike cholesterol, glucose or calcium there is **no internationally recognized gold-standard method** to which labs must peg their fatty-acid assays. Example: EPA+DHA "might be 6.7% in Lab A and 5.2% in Lab B". — [OmegaQuant FAQ](https://omegaquant.com/frequent-question/how-is-the-omega-3-index-different-from-other-fatty-acid-profile-tests/)
- von Schacky (Proc Nutr Soc 2020) notes that results from other methods are "frequently related to the target range of 8–11%, defined for the original and scientifically validated method (HS-Omega-3 Index®)", which raises "ethical issues", and calls for standardization. Author affiliation: Omegametrix (conflict of interest). — [Proc Nutr Soc 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-nutrition-society/article/omega3-index-in-201819/8576C6B755BA9697A62A981E905D5DD7)

**GC-FID vs. GC-MS, peak identification**
- GC-MS (electron impact) quantification of FAMEs "compared satisfactorily" with GC-FID and adds spectral confirmation of peak identity (Lipids 2005). — [PubMed 16028722](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16028722/)
- FID response depends on the number of C–H carbons, and oxygen-containing groups reduce it. Theoretical or empirical response-factor corrections are needed, after which FID and MS agree. — [IntechOpen chapter](https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/46047)
- GOED industry advisory: response factors for EPA and DHA should be close to 1.0, and values outside about 0.90–1.10 require corrective action. — [GOED advisory](https://goedomega3.com/storage/app/media/technical%20reports/goed-advisory-quantification-of-epa-dha-and-total-omega-3.pdf)
- With GC-FID, retention time is the only identifier, so co-elution is a limitation. MS offers greater selectivity. — [CSU thesis, GC-FID vs. GC-MS](https://scholarworks.calstate.edu/downloads/b5644t56c)

### Inferences
- Denominator arithmetic (illustrative, my own calculation): if a lab counts only the 11 most abundant fatty acids, which make up about 90–95% of the total mass, every reported % is inflated by about 5–10% relative compared with a 26-FA panel (e.g., an O3I of 6.0% would appear as about 6.3–6.6%). Omitting minor peaks, or mis-assigning a co-eluting peak (e.g., 20:5n-3 vs. 20:4n-3, or 22:6n-3 vs. 24:1), shifts the numerator directly. The 3.5-fold historical spread suggests that much larger errors occurred in practice (integration, identification, matrix, oxidation).
- The panel size and composition (e.g., "26 fatty acids"), GC detector type, calibration standards, response factors and participation in proficiency testing are the key technical facts a test provider should disclose. A provider that does not disclose them cannot claim comparability with the RBC literature.

### Gaps
- The full list and number of fatty acids used by individual commercial DBS labs could not be checked in the scientific literature (company specifics excluded by scope).
- No primary publication of the original "3.5-fold" five-lab experiment (dates, labs, samples) was found; it is known only from von Schacky's reviews.
- No study quantifying the effect of 11 vs. 24–26 FA denominators on the Omega-3 Index was retrieved; the inference above is arithmetic, not empirical.

## 5. Precision: analytical CVs (DBS vs. RBC), within-person biological variability, minimum meaningful change (reference change value)

### Takeaway
Good RBC and DBS methods reach analytical CVs of about 3.5–5% for EPA+DHA, and within-person biological CV of the RBC Omega-3 Index is about 4% over 6 weeks (plasma about 15–16%). By standard reference-change-value arithmetic, two Omega-3 Index results from the *same* lab must differ by roughly **≥11–16% relative (≈ 0.7–1.0 percentage points at an index of 6%)** before a change is likely real. Changes between labs or methods cannot be interpreted.

### Cited Findings
- Harris & Polreis 2016 (DBS): in-lab inter-assay **CV 3.6%** at an Omega-3 Index of 7.9% and **4.9%** at 4.6%. CV was < 5% in a Mexican field study. — [Harris & Polreis 2016](https://omegaquant.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/12/DBS-Method-paper-WSH-JP-2016-Ann-Clin-Lab-Res.pdf)
- HS-Omega-3 Index (RBC): analytical variability "**4 relative %**", described as acceptable in clinical chemistry (von Schacky 2014). A trial record cites a **CV of 5%** for EPA+DHA with quality assured according to DIN ISO 15189. — [von Schacky 2014, LaboratoriumsMedizin](https://doi.org/10.1515/labmed-2014-0007); [NCT01089231](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT01089231)
- Inter-lab CVs for DBS EPA/DHA across 5 labs were 6.0–10.5% depending on reporting (Metherel 2019; see section 4). — [Metherel et al. 2019](https://aocs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/lipd.12203)
- Within-subject biological CV (6 weekly samples, n = 20): **RBC EPA+DHA 4.1 ± 1.9%**, plasma 15.9 ± 6.4%, plasma PL 14.5 ± 8.4% (Harris & Thomas 2010). A 2013 follow-up summarizes plasma as having "4 times greater biological variability". — [Harris & Thomas 2010](https://ohsu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/biological-variability-of-blood-omega-3-biomarkers-2/); [J Clin Lipidol 2013](https://www.lipidjournal.com/article/S1933-2874(13)00198-0/fulltext)
- Whole-blood biological variability is given as about 6–7% in a von Schacky editorial (snippet; verify). — [von Schacky J Clin Lipidol 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1933287424002708)
- A CV of 6% for the Omega-3 Index was reported in a sudden-cardiac-arrest study (origin unclear). — [Lipids 2011, PMC3038230](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3038230/)
- Response to supplementation varies widely between individuals (response CV ≈ 0.29–0.32 in whole blood/plasma; Sparkes 2020). Genetics, BMI (if RBC EPA+DHA < 6%), smoking and alcohol were negatively associated with levels. — [Sparkes et al. 2020](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC7231102/)

### Inferences (own calculations using the standard reference-change-value formula RCV = √2 × 1.96 × √(CVa² + CVi²))
- **RBC Omega-3 Index, same lab:** CVa 4%, CVi 4.1% → RCV ≈ 2.77 × 5.7% ≈ **16% relative**. Because the Harris & Thomas CVi of 4.1% probably already includes analytical noise, a lower bound is 2.77 × 4.1% ≈ **11%**. At an index of 6%, a change must be about **±0.7–1.0 percentage points** to be significant at 95%.
- **DBS whole blood, same lab:** CVa about 4–5%, CVi about 6–7% (if the von Schacky estimate holds) → RCV ≈ 2.77 × 7.5–8.6% ≈ **21–24% relative**. At a whole-blood EPA+DHA of 5%, that is about ±1.1–1.2 percentage points. If a converted "Omega-3 Index" is reported, the individual conversion uncertainty (about ±1.2 percentage points; section 2 inference) adds to this.
- **Plasma:** CVi about 15–16% → RCV ≈ **43–45% relative**, which is unsuitable for monitoring individuals.
- **Between labs:** inter-lab CVs of 6–10% even under ring-trial conditions, plus historical 3.5-fold differences, mean that a change from a test at lab A to a test at lab B cannot be interpreted.
- For n-6:n-3 and AA:EPA *ratios*, the errors of numerator and denominator combine, and a small EPA denominator (often < 1% of fatty acids) carries high relative CV (EPA was the only fatty acid with inter-lab variability > 30% in NIST 2017; section 6). These ratios are therefore expected to have markedly larger CVs and RCVs than EPA+DHA. *This is inferred, not measured.*

### Gaps
- No published reference change value or least significant change for the Omega-3 Index was found, and no long-term (months to years) ICC for RBC EPA+DHA was retrieved.
- No published within-person CV for DBS-derived AA:EPA or n-6:n-3 ratios was found.

## 6. Inter-laboratory comparability and standardization: NIST FAQAP, HS-Omega-3 Index vs. Omega-3 Index, harmonization calls (2017–2025)

### Takeaway
There is **no certified reference method** for blood fatty-acid percentages. The only public proficiency-testing effort, NIST FAQAP (serum/plasma, 2012–2017, later part of HAMQAP), found inter-lab agreement within 20% for only **70%** of results, and inter-lab variability **> 30% for EPA** in 2017. The HS-Omega-3 Index (Omegametrix/von Schacky; 26 FA, RBC, CV about 4%, proficiency-tested among the licensed labs) is a *proprietary* standardization. The OmegaQuant (Harris) "Omega-3 Index" grew from the same origin but is a separate lab method. No published reference material or ring trial covers commercial DBS tests in general.

### Cited Findings
- NIST/NIH ODS/CDC **Fatty Acid Quality Assurance Program (FAQAP)**, founded 2012: 24 fatty acids in serum/plasma. First two exercises had 11 and 14 participants. **Agreement among labs for individual fatty acids was within 20% for 70% of the data**, and within-lab RSD was < 20%. — [Interlaboratory comparison, PubMed 27662814](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27662814/); [NIST program page](https://www.nist.gov/programs-projects/fatty-acids-human-serum-and-plasma-quality-assurance-program-faqap)
- NISTIR 8273 (2017 exercise, published 2019; 14 participants; SRM 1950 as control, three unknown sera): "interlaboratory variability for all FAs but **EPA** were less than 30%". FAQAP was folded into the Health Assessment Measurements QAP (HAMQAP) in 2017. — [NISTIR 8273](https://nvlpubs.nist.gov/nistpubs/ir/2019/NIST.IR.8273.pdf)
- SRM 2378 (fatty acids in frozen human serum) provides certified EPA/DHA values. Its certificate notes that inter-lab variability required further exercises. — [SRM 2378 certificate](https://stacks.cdc.gov/view/cdc/52550/cdc_52550_DS1.pdf)
- A 2020 review discusses lipid stability during sample preparation and standardization efforts for lipid reporting. — [PMC7725844](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7725844/)
- HS-Omega-3 Index: the method was installed in **three laboratories (US, Germany, Korea)** and "successfully subjected to an inter-laboratory comparison, i.e., proficiency testing". Analytical CV is about 4%. — [von Schacky 2014 (PDF)](https://pdfs.semanticscholar.org/1d2a/fea5fb5a3fb9d691b51a70026cc45e61c5c3.pdf); [Proc Nutr Soc 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-nutrition-society/article/omega3-index-in-201819/8576C6B755BA9697A62A981E905D5DD7)
- The name "HS-Omega-3 Index" was also used by Harris/OmegaQuant around 2013 ("The HS-Omega-3 Index Methodological Considerations"). This shows the shared origin of the two brands. — [OmegaQuant methodological document](https://omegaquant.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/09/omegaquant_methodological.pdf)
- DBS ring trial (5 labs, 2 subjects; Metherel 2019): standardizing to 19 FA lowered inter-lab CVs to about 6–7% for EPA and DHA. — [Metherel et al. 2019](https://aocs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/lipd.12203)
- von Schacky (2020, 2025) and the J Nutr 2024 review call for standardization or harmonization and warn against using target ranges validated with one method for results from another method. — [Proc Nutr Soc 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-nutrition-society/article/omega3-index-in-201819/8576C6B755BA9697A62A981E905D5DD7); [J Nutr 2024](https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(24)00164-0/fulltext); [J Clin Lipidol 2025](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1933287424002708)

### Inferences
- In 2026, "accuracy" for omega-3 tests can only mean *internal* consistency of one lab plus traceability to the cohort data behind its cut-offs. There is no external truth standard comparable to cholesterol (CDC LSP) or HbA1c (IFCC/NGSP).
- The strongest comparability claim belongs to a method that (a) uses the same matrix as the risk studies (RBC), (b) uses the same fatty-acid panel and method as those studies, and (c) participates in documented proficiency testing. DBS whole-blood methods need an additional validated conversion step (section 2).

### Gaps
- The proficiency-testing data of the HS-Omega-3 Index network (number of rounds, between-lab CVs) are not published in detail. One search snippet indicated that a review called parts of the method and validation proprietary, but the attribution could not be verified.
- No ISO/IFCC reference measurement procedure or external quality assessment (EQA) scheme for DBS omega-3 tests in Europe (e.g., INSTAND, RfB) was found in the retrieved sources (not specifically searched).

## 7. Validity of metrics: Omega-3 Index (risk-marker evidence, targets), omega-6:omega-3 ratio, AA:EPA ratio, before/after designs and regression to the mean

### Takeaway
The RBC Omega-3 Index has consistent prospective-cohort support: about 15–18% lower all-cause mortality in the top vs. bottom quintile (FORCE 2021, 17 cohorts), and about 35% lower fatal CHD risk at 8% vs. 4%. The 8–11% target is an expert-derived range, not an RCT-proven treatment goal. The omega-6:omega-3 ratio has been called "scientifically out-dated" (Harris 2018) and "not a useful concept" (Harris at the UK FSA workshop 2007), and the AHA (2009) supports at least 5–10% of energy from omega-6. AA:EPA has some cohort support, but mostly in Japanese or high-CRP settings, with context-dependent results. Uncontrolled before/after test designs are vulnerable to regression to the mean, pre-analytical bias and lab-method changes.

### Cited Findings
**Omega-3 Index as a risk marker**
- Harris WS & von Schacky C. Prev Med 2004;39:212–220: proposed RBC EPA+DHA as a risk factor for CHD death. An index **≥ 8%** was associated with the greatest cardioprotection and **≤ 4%** with the least. — [Harris & von Schacky 2004 (EPA HERO)](https://hero.epa.gov/reference/7130342/)
- Harris, Del Gobbo, Tintle. Atherosclerosis 2017;262:51–54 (10 cohorts from the Del Gobbo meta-analysis, translated into O3I equivalents): mean Omega-3 Index **6.1% (SD 2.1%)**; **HR for fatal CHD per 1-SD increase 0.85 (95% CI 0.80–0.91)**. Medians in the compared quintiles were 4.2% vs. 8.3%, which corresponds to about **35% lower fatal CHD risk at 8% vs. 4%**. — [Atherosclerosis 2017](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S002191501730196X); [summary of the 35% figure](https://jn.nutrition.org/article/S0022-3166(22)10949-1/fulltext)
- **FORCE consortium**, Harris WS et al. Nat Commun 2021;12:2329: de-novo pooled analysis of **17 prospective cohorts** (about 42,466 people, mean follow-up about 16 years, 15,720 deaths). Higher EPA, DPA, DHA and EPA+DHA were associated with **15–18% lower all-cause mortality** (top vs. bottom quintile; trend p < 0.01). Associations were inverse with CVD, cancer and other mortality. No association was found for ALA. One secondary snippet reports HR 0.87 (0.83–0.90) for EPA+DHA (verify). — [Nature Communications 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41467-021-22370-2)
- Framingham Offspring (Harris, Tintle, Etherton, Vasan; J Clin Lipidol 2018; n ≈ 2,500, median follow-up 7.3 years): Omega-3 Index **> 6.8% vs. < 4.2%** → **34% lower all-cause mortality** and 39% lower incident CVD (p-trend for mortality 0.02). Limitations: single exposure measurement and short follow-up. — [Framingham 2018, PMC6034629](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC6034629/)
- 8–11% target range: von Schacky states it is "defined for the original and scientifically validated method (HS-Omega-3 Index®)". A secondary source describes about 8% as an average that "seemed like a reasonable target" to Harris and von Schacky, i.e., expert-derived. — [Proc Nutr Soc 2020](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/proceedings-of-the-nutrition-society/article/omega3-index-in-201819/8576C6B755BA9697A62A981E905D5DD7); [OmegaQuant series part 1](https://omegaquant.com/discovering-the-omega-3-index/)

**Omega-6:omega-3 ratio**
- Harris WS. Curr Atheroscler Rep 2006;8:453–9 ("uses and abuses"): the ratio "may be of value" for interpreting biomarkers and recommendations, but "there are few human experimental and clinical trial data to support this view". A 2007 version (Curr Cardiovasc Risk Rep) concludes that metrics including n-3 fatty acids alone, especially EPA+DHA, "appear to hold the greatest promise". — [PubMed 17045070](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17045070/); [Curr Cardiovasc Risk Rep 2007](https://ohsu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/the-omega-6omega-3-ratio-and-cardiovascular-disease-risk-uses-and-3/)
- UK Food Standards Agency workshop report (Br J Nutr 2007): Harris concluded that the n-6:n-3 ratio "is not a useful concept" and distracts from increasing absolute intakes of very-long-chain n-3 fatty acids. — [PubMed 18039412](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/18039412/)
- Harris WS. PLEFA 2018;132:34–40, "The Omega-6:Omega-3 ratio: A critical appraisal and possible successor": the ratio "has become scientifically out-dated". The paper proposes the Omega-3 Index as its successor. — [PubMed 29599053](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29599053/)
- AHA Science Advisory (Harris WS, Mozaffarian D, Rimm E et al. Circulation 2009;119:902–907): supports an omega-6 PUFA intake of **at least 5–10% of energy**. Observational data link higher omega-6 intake with lower CHD incidence, and in a meta-analysis of 6 RCTs, replacing saturated fat with PUFA lowered CHD events by 24%. A related AHA commentary notes that expert "optimal ratio" opinions ranged from 1:1 to 1:20. — [AHA Top Things to Know](https://professional.heart.org/en/science-news/omega-6-fatty-acids-and-risk-for-cardiovascular-disease%5Ctop-things-to-know); [EurekAlert 2009](https://www.eurekalert.org/pub_releases/2009-01/aha-ofa012609.php)
- The 2025 global DBS dataset reports whole-blood total n-6:n-3 ratios of 6.2:1–8.8:1 by continent. Even supplement users remain above the levels typically associated with omega-3 supplementation (company-linked dataset). — [Lipids Health Dis 2025](https://lipidworld.biomedcentral.com/articles/10.1186/s12944-025-02676-6)
- Whole-blood n-6:n-3 ratios are systematically higher than RBC ratios (Bell 2011), so ratio targets are matrix-dependent. — [Bell et al. 2011](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/british-journal-of-nutrition/article/using-a-fingertip-whole-blood-sample-for-rapid-fatty-acid-measurement-method-validation-and-correlation-with-erythrocyte-polar-lipid-compositions-in-uk-subjects/016380E588A2B7FF348283D63AC1054F)

**AA:EPA (or EPA:AA) ratio**
- A 2019 review (Postgrad Med) calls EPA:AA a robust marker of future CV events "in a number of clinical settings" (mostly Japanese data). It notes that regional diet differences affect both the ratio and baseline risk. — [Postgrad Med 2019](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00325481.2019.1607414)
- Hisayama Study (Atherosclerosis 2013): serum EPA/AA was associated with CVD risk **only in people with hs-CRP ≥ 1.0 mg/L**. There was no association at lower CRP and none for DHA/AA. — [PubMed 24267237](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/24267237/)
- A 2026 coronary-artery-disease cohort found lower MACE risk at a higher free EPA/AA ratio, but the association weakened in sensitivity analyses. — [Frontiers Cardiovasc Med 2026](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/cardiovascular-medicine/articles/10.3389/fcvm.2026.1944383/full)

**Before/after designs, regression to the mean**
- Barnett AG, van der Pols JC, Dobson AJ. Int J Epidemiol 2005;34:215–220: regression to the mean (RTM) makes natural variation look like real change. It grows with measurement error and when follow-up is restricted to people selected for extreme baseline values. A control group, or baseline adjustment such as ANCOVA, is needed. — [PubMed 15333621](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15333621/)
- Kelly & Price 2005 (Am Nat): RTM affects both experimental and control groups, so a well-designed controlled study is not biased by it. Formula-based corrections without controls are disputed (arXiv 2025). — [Kelly & Price 2005](https://www.zoology.ubc.ca/~bio501/R/readings/kelly%20&%20price%202005%20am%20nat%20-%20regression%20toward%20the%20mean.pdf); [arXiv 2509.04718](https://arxiv.org/html/2509.04718v1)
- RBC EPA reaches steady state only after about 180 days (half-life about 28 days; Katan 1997). Re-testing after about 120 days therefore captures most, but not all, of an intervention effect for RBC. Whole blood also reflects recent intake. — [PubMed 9374124](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9374124/)

### Inferences
- Validity hierarchy for the report: (1) RBC EPA+DHA (Omega-3 Index) measured with the method used in the cohorts has the best prognostic evidence, although it is observational and RCTs of supplementation on hard endpoints are mixed (not covered here). (2) DBS-derived "Omega-3 Index" values are valid only to the extent their conversion is validated (section 2). (3) Ratios (n-6:n-3, AA:EPA) are not established clinical targets in Western guidelines. They are matrix- and lab-dependent, numerically unstable when EPA is small, and confound "too much omega-6" with "too little omega-3". A "balance" target such as ≤3:1 has no outcome-based validation in the retrieved literature.
- A before/after design (test → supplement → retest after about 120 days) *will* show improvement in most users, partly through a genuine effect (EPA+DHA intake does raise levels), but also through several biases:
  - RTM, especially when people test because they suspect a deficiency, or when the first result was extreme.
  - Oxidative losses in the first sample, if handling differed.
  - Lab or panel changes between tests.
  - Recent intake before the second whole-blood sample.
  The size of the change says nothing about the test's accuracy. Credible evidence requires a randomized control group, the same lab, the same panel and the same pre-analytical conditions, and changes larger than the RCV (section 5).
- An "improvement" in the n-6:n-3 or AA:EPA ratio achieved mostly by raising EPA is mathematically large because EPA sits in the denominator and starts from a low level. This makes such ratios attractive for marketing even though the underlying clinical evidence favours the absolute EPA+DHA value.

### Gaps
- EFSA and ESC positions on the Omega-3 Index as a biomarker were not retrieved in this session. EFSA has set an adequate intake (AI) of 250 mg EPA+DHA/day for adults (2010), but that was not re-verified here, and no EFSA or ESC endorsement of the Omega-3 Index or of ratio targets was found.
- The exact wording of the AHA 2009 advisory on the n-6:n-3 ratio could not be read in full text. The abstract statement that lowering omega-6 intake "would be more likely to increase than to decrease risk for CHD" is from memory of the abstract and should be verified at DOI 10.1161/CIRCULATIONAHA.108.191627.
- No meta-analysis specific to AA:EPA in Western populations, and no independent critique of whole-blood ratio targets (e.g., "≤ 3:1"), were found.
- A newer cross-sectional analysis of 762,150 DBS samples (supplementation patterns; likely the same company-linked dataset) was seen only as a title. — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/415929230_Real-world_omega-3_supplementation_patterns_and_whole-blood_fatty-acid_biomarkers_a_cross-sectional_analysis_of_762150_dried_blood_spot_samples)

### Yardstick (summary checklist for judging the three consumer tests)
Derived from the findings above; the inferences are the author's.
1. **Matrix:** is the reported value RBC EPA+DHA (Omega-3 Index proper) or a whole-blood value? If whole blood, is it converted, with which equation, and with what validation (n, r/R², bias, limits of agreement)? An r of about 0.96 means an individual uncertainty of about ±1.2 percentage points.
2. **Pre-analytics:** antioxidant-treated card? Documented room-temperature and heat stability? Maximum transit time? Without antioxidant, significant losses occur by day 3; with BHT, about −19% EPA+DHA by day 28.
3. **Panel and denominator:** number and identity of fatty acids (e.g., 26 for the HS-O3I), GC method, response factors.
4. **Precision:** published analytical CV (benchmark ≤ 5%), and reporting of a minimum meaningful change (≈ ≥ 0.7–1.0 percentage points for RBC O3I; ≥ about 1.1–1.2 percentage points or more for DBS).
5. **External QA:** proficiency testing or ring-trial participation; traceability of target ranges to cohort data measured *with the same method*.
6. **Metrics:** is the primary result EPA+DHA (evidence-backed), or a ratio (weakly validated, matrix-dependent)?
7. **Study claims:** are before/after "improvement" statistics controlled for RTM, lab or panel changes and pre-analytics?
