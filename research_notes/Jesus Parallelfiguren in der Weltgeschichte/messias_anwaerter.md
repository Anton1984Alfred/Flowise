### Inferences

## 2. Ancient Jewish messianic claimants and popular prophets (mostly from Josephus)

### Takeaway
Josephus names roughly a dozen would-be kings, sign prophets and rebel leaders between Herod's death (4 BCE) and the Bar Kokhba revolt (132–135 CE). Most were killed by Rome or in battle. Scholars disagree on which of them were "messianic" in the strict sense. Horsley & Hanson's *Bandits, Prophets, and Messiahs* (1985) splits them into royal pretenders and Moses-like sign prophets.

### Cited Findings
- After Herod's death (4 BCE), three "royal pretenders" appeared: **Judas son of Hezekiah, Simon of Peraea and Athronges**. Josephus gives Athronges the most royal language (he wore a crown). Simon of Peraea "likely claimed some messianic status" — [CDAMM: Early Jewish Messiahs](https://www.cdamm.org/articles/early-jewish-messiahs); [A. Jacobs, course handout "Varieties of Ancient Judaism"](https://andrewjacobs.org/ancientjudaism/handout4c.pdf)
- **Judas of Galilee** led the revolt against the Roman census (6 CE) and is considered the founder of the Zealots. His son or grandson **Menahem** made messianic claims during the Jewish War (66 CE) — [A. Jacobs handout](https://andrewjacobs.org/ancientjudaism/handout4c.pdf); [Wikipedia: Judas of Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judas_of_Galilee)
- **Theudas** and **"the Egyptian"** are described by Josephus as prophets claiming a Moses/Joshua role (Theudas: parting the Jordan; the Egyptian: making the walls of Jerusalem fall). Theudas was captured and executed by the Roman procurator — [Vridar](https://vridar.org/2016/07/29/questioning-carrier-and-the-myth-that-the-jews-expected-a-messiah-3/); [A. Jacobs handout](https://andrewjacobs.org/ancientjudaism/handout4c.pdf)
- Key Josephus passages: *Jewish War* 2.55–65 and 2.259–263; *Antiquities* 17.271–284, 20.97–98, 20.169–171 — [Search summary of the secondary literature](https://historicalchristian.faith/doctrine/doctrines/messianic-claimants/arguments/who-counts-as-messianic.html)
- Scholarly disagreement: Horsley argues the post-Herod pretenders were later seen as messianic. Critics (Vridar, building on Novenson) say calling Theudas/the Egyptian "messianic claimants" blurs Josephus' own distinction between sign prophets and royal pretenders — [Vridar](https://vridar.org/2016/07/29/questioning-carrier-and-the-myth-that-the-jews-expected-a-messiah-3/); [historicalchristian.faith](https://historicalchristian.faith/doctrine/doctrines/messianic-claimants/arguments/who-counts-as-messianic.html)
- **Simon bar Kokhba**: revolt 132–135 CE against Hadrian (triggers: Roman colony planned on the site of Jerusalem, restrictions such as a ban on circumcision). Rabbi Akiva hailed him as Messiah and gave him the title "Bar Kokhba" ("Son of the Star", cf. Num 24:17). The revolt was crushed in 135 after the fall of Bethar — [Britannica: Bar Kokhba](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Bar-Kokhba-Jewish-leader); [Britannica: Bar Kokhba Revolt](https://www.britannica.com/event/Bar-Kokhba-Revolt)
- **Moses of Crete** (5th century, c. 440–470): promised to lead the Jews of Crete dry-shod through the sea to the Promised Land. His fate is disputed: some accounts say he fled, others that he died in the sea; many followers drowned — [Wikipedia: List of Jewish messiah claimants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Jewish_messiah_claimants); [Forward: Six Failed Messiahs](https://forward.com/culture/346956/six-failed-messiahs-from-jewish-history/); [Kotzk Blog](https://www.kotzkblog.com/2022/03/377-early-jewish-messiahs-and-their.html)

### Inferences
- Count (approximate, depends on definition): around 9–12 named figures between 4 BCE and 135 CE (Judas b. Hezekiah, Simon of Peraea, Athronges, Judas the Galilean, Theudas, the Egyptian, Menahem, Simon bar Giora, Bar Kokhba; plus, from background knowledge, the Samaritan prophet of 36 CE and Jonathan the weaver of Cyrene, c. 73 CE). Moses of Crete follows in the 5th century.
- Parallel to Jesus: all were active in the same Roman-occupied, expectation-filled setting; several gathered followers in the wilderness or at the Jordan or the Mount of Olives (as Jesus did), promised signs, and were executed or killed by Roman forces. The difference is that most were military or royal pretenders aiming at a political kingdom, and no lasting movement claimed their resurrection.

### Gaps
- Background knowledge, NOT verified against a fetched source in this session (Josephus/Acts are the primary sources):
  - Theudas was beheaded under the procurator Cuspius Fadus (c. 44–46 CE).
  - The Egyptian (c. 52–58 CE, under Felix) gathered followers on the Mount of Olives (Josephus gives 30,000; Acts 21:38 gives 4,000). Roman troops attacked them; he escaped.
  - Simon of Peraea was killed by Gratus' troops. Athronges and his brothers were eventually defeated.
  - Menahem was murdered by a rival faction in Jerusalem in 66 CE.
  - Simon bar Giora was led in Titus' triumph in Rome in 71 CE and executed.
  - Acts 5:36–37 (Gamaliel's speech) itself compares the Jesus movement with Theudas and Judas the Galilean. This is a striking ancient "parallel figures" passage.
- The cause and number of the Moses of Crete deaths are unclear and the sources conflict.

## 3. Medieval and early modern claimants (Jewish, Christian, Islamic, Chinese)

### Takeaway
After antiquity, Jewish messianic claimants appear every few centuries. The largest movement was Sabbatai Zevi's in 1665–66: tens of thousands of followers, then mass disillusion after his conversion to Islam. Christian "new Christs" range from medieval heretics to Shaker founder Ann Lee. Islamic Mahdi claimants founded states (the Sudanese Mahdiyya) or lasting communities (Ahmadiyya). Hong Xiuquan's claim to be Jesus' younger brother led to one of the deadliest wars in history (estimate about 20 million dead).

### Cited Findings
- **David Alroy** (born in Kurdistan) declared himself Messiah around 1160 — [Jewish Encyclopedia: Pseudo-Messiahs](https://jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/12416-pseudo-messiahs). One popular account describes him as a man who revolted against the heavy caliphate taxes on Jews — [Forward](https://forward.com/culture/346956/six-failed-messiahs-from-jewish-history/)
- **Sabbatai Zevi** (1626–1676): on 16 Sept 1666, given a choice between execution and conversion, he converted to Islam. About **300 families** converted with him and became the **Dönme** — [Wikipedia: Sabbatai Zevi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabbatai_Zevi); [JewAge](https://www.jewage.org/wiki/en/Article:Sabbatai_Zevi_-_Biography)
- Before the conversion, his following numbered "tens of thousands of Jews" — [Jewish Rhody: How 1666 helps explain 2017](https://www.jewishrhody.com/stories/how-1666-helps-explain-2017,7652)
- Scholem: even the conversion "failed to shake many of his followers"; large numbers, especially Sephardim, stayed loyal — [Commentary (Scholem excerpt)](https://www.commentary.org/articles/mati-meged/sabbatai-zevi-by-gershom-scholem/); [Commentary: The Holiness of Sin](https://www.commentary.org/articles/gershom-scholem/the-holiness-of-sin/). Other accounts say most followers abandoned their belief — [Ynet](https://www.ynetnews.com/jewish-world/article/sjr9e2bhge)
- **Hong Xiuquan** (Taiping): influenced by Christian tracts, he believed himself to be the son of God and younger brother of Jesus Christ, sent to reform China — [Britannica: Hong Xiuquan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hong-Xiuquan)
- Taiping Rebellion (1850–1864) death toll: Britannica gives about **20 million**. Other estimates run from 20–30 million up to speculative figures of 50–70 million or more — [Britannica: Taiping Rebellion](https://www.britannica.com/event/Taiping-Rebellion); [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/taiping-rebellion); [TheCollector](https://www.thecollector.com/why-taiping-rebellion-surpassed-world-war-one-casualties/)
- Hong died in June 1864. Suicide versus illness or poisoning is disputed. Nanjing fell in July 1864 — [Britannica: Hong Xiuquan](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hong-Xiuquan); [History.com](https://www.history.com/articles/taiping-rebellion)
- **Muhammad Ahmad** (Sudan) proclaimed himself Mahdi in June 1881 and led a successful campaign against the Turco-Egyptian government. Khartoum fell on 26 January 1885 (or the night of 25–26 Jan) and General Gordon was killed. He died in June 1885, probably of typhus — [World History Encyclopedia: Mahdist War](https://www.worldhistory.org/Mahdist_War/); [EBSCO Research Starters: The Mahdi](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/history/mahdi/); [Wikipedia: Mahdist State](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahdist_State)
- **Mirza Ghulam Ahmad** (founder of the Ahmadiyya, India): from 1891 he claimed to be the Promised Messiah and Mahdi. Ahmadis revere him as such. His claim to be a subordinate (ummati) prophet remains highly controversial in Islam — [Wikipedia: Mirza Ghulam Ahmad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirza_Ghulam_Ahmad); [Wikipedia: The Heavenly Decree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Heavenly_Decree)
- **John Nichols Thom** ("Sir William Courtenay", Kent, England): in 1834 he claimed to be the "saviour of the world" and the reincarnation of Jesus. He was killed by British soldiers at the **Battle of Bossenden Wood on 31 May 1838** — [The Fuel Project (blog list; weak source)](https://thefuelproject.org/blog/2018/3/2/list-of-people-who-claimed-to-be-jesus); he is also on the [Wikipedia list](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_people_claimed_to_be_Jesus)
- **Ann Lee** (18th century) is listed as founder of the Shakers — [The Fuel Project](https://thefuelproject.org/blog/2018/3/2/list-of-people-who-claimed-to-be-jesus) (no detailed source found)

### Inferences
- Sabbatai Zevi has the closest structural parallel to Jesus among Jewish claimants: a charismatic figure, a prophet-herald (Nathan of Gaza, comparable to John the Baptist), a mass movement across the diaspora, and a crisis (here conversion instead of crucifixion). Followers reinterpreted the crisis theologically, and believers survived after his death (Dönme). This mirrors how early Christians reinterpreted the cross.
- Hong Xiuquan is the most extreme case of consciously appropriating the Jesus story (Jesus' "younger brother"). He had the largest real-world impact of any claimant, with tens of millions dead.
- Mirza Ghulam Ahmad is the clearest example of someone claiming to *be* the returned Messiah/Jesus figure within Islam (the Islamic expectation of the return of 'Isa).

### Gaps
- No source found in this session for: the commonly cited "~1 million followers" of Sabbatai Zevi (only "tens of thousands" and "300 families" found). Do not print 1 million without a source, or present it as a rough and disputed estimate.
- No sources fetched on **Jacob Frank** (1726–1791). Background knowledge, unverified: he presented himself as a reincarnation of Sabbatai Zevi; he and followers were baptized Catholic in Poland in 1759; he was imprisoned at Częstochowa 1760–1773.
- No sources fetched on **Abraham Abulafia** (b. 1240). Background: he declared himself Messiah around 1280 and tried to see Pope Nicholas III.
- No sources fetched on **Tanchelm of Antwerp** (d. 1115; claimed divine status, reportedly "married" a statue of the Virgin Mary) or **Eudo of Brittany / Éon de l'Étoile** (d. c. 1150; claimed to be "he who shall come to judge the living and the dead"; condemned at the Council of Reims 1148, died in prison). Both are background knowledge, unverified.
- **Ann Lee** (1736–1784): background knowledge, unverified. Followers regarded her as the female embodiment of Christ's second coming ("Mother Ann"). The Shakers emigrated from Manchester to America in 1774.
- **Jemima Wilkinson** (1752–1819, the "Public Universal Friend"): no source found. Background: after a 1776 illness she claimed to be a genderless spirit animating her body. Some followers regarded her as a messiah.
- Mirza Ghulam Ahmad's specific Jesus theology (Jesus survived the crucifixion and died in Kashmir; he himself came "in the spirit and power" of Jesus) is background knowledge, not sourced here.
- Juhayman al-Otaibi (1979): the 2006 mirror says "son-in-law". Standard accounts name his brother-in-law Muhammad Abdullah al-Qahtani as the proclaimed Mahdi. This is unverified here.

## 4. Modern (19th–21st century) "I am Jesus" / messiah figures and their current status (as of Nov 2026)

### Takeaway
Since the 20th century, self-declared Christs have mostly been new-religious-movement leaders. Several ended in mass death (Jonestown 918 dead, Waco about 76–80, Shakahola 429+ bodies), and several living claimants were in prison or facing trial in 2025–26. Vissarion was sentenced to 12 years in June 2025. Quiboloy is in Philippine detention and faced a US extradition warrant in Nov 2026. Mackenzie (Shakahola) was sentenced to 200 years on 6 Nov 2026.

### Inferences
- Pattern of modern claimants: (a) small online or rural communities (Inri Cristo, AJ Miller, Vissarion's "City of the Sun"); (b) authoritarian sects with sexual abuse and financial-crime charges (Quiboloy, Jung Myung-seok, Vissarion); (c) apocalyptic sects that ended in mass death (Jonestown, Waco, Shakahola). Category (c) "reproduces" the Passion in a destructive way: the leader and followers die, framed as martyrdom or a "passage to heaven". The Shakahola judge explicitly noted that the doctrine treated death "as a triumphant passage".
- Courts now treat such claimants as ordinary criminals (Vissarion 12 years, Mackenzie 200 years). Unlike Jesus and the ancient pretenders, the state's response is a criminal trial rather than crucifixion or military suppression.
- Mackenzie is better described as an end-times prophet (a starvation-fasting cult "to meet Jesus") than as a self-declared Jesus. The report should not label him "the man who claimed to be Jesus" without a source.

## 5. Psychiatry: Jerusalem syndrome and "I am Jesus/Messiah" delusions

### Takeaway
Jerusalem syndrome is a documented but rare phenomenon. The Jerusalem group (Bar-El et al.) reported around 50 tourist hospitalizations per year in earlier work, and the 2000 paper (BJP 176:86–90) proposed a typology in which only a small subset (42 cases over 13 years) had no prior psychiatric illness. Religious delusions in general are common in schizophrenia (about a quarter of English inpatients), but there are no good figures for how many specifically believe they *are* Jesus. Rokeach's "Three Christs" is a famous case study, not a prevalence study.

### Cited Findings
- Bar-El Y, Durst R, Katz G, Zislin J, Strauss Z, Knobler HY. "Jerusalem syndrome." *British Journal of Psychiatry* 2000; 176:86–90 (DOI 10.1192/bjp.176.1.86) — [ResearchGate entry](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/274055647_Jerusalem_syndrome); [ResearchGate (PDF entry)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12528691_Jerusalem_syndrome)
- An earlier 1991 study by the Jerusalem group reported about **50 tourists hospitalized per year** after psychotic episodes connected with Jerusalem — [ResearchGate: The Jerusalem Syndrome](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/12407286_The_Jerusalem_Syndrome)
- Per Wikipedia, the 2000 paper described **42 cases over 13 years** of the "pure" form (no previous psychiatric history), and the transience could not be confirmed (the statement is tagged "citation needed") — [Wikipedia: Jerusalem syndrome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerusalem_syndrome)
- Kalian and Witztum criticized the paper in the BJP for giving no epidemiological data for its typology, and said such data were also missing in earlier studies — [Cambridge Core: Comments on Jerusalem syndrome](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/the-british-journal-of-psychiatry/article/comments-on-jerusalem-syndrome/204D416E036D67857156730807F3E4B7)
- The paper predicted a rise in cases around the millennium (2000). - **Rokeach, *The Three Christs of Ypsilanti* (1964)**: at Ypsilanti State Hospital (Michigan), he brought together three men diagnosed with paranoid schizophrenia who each claimed to be Jesus Christ: Clyde Benson, Joseph Cassel and Leon Gabor. - **Siddle et al. 2002** (*Social Psychiatry and Psychiatric Epidemiology* 37:130–138): of **193** inpatients with schizophrenia in England, **24%** had religious delusions. These patients were more severely ill (higher symptom scores, lower GAF, more medication) — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s001270200005); [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/11380319_Religious_delusions_in_patients_admitted_to_hospital_with_schizophrenia)
- Rates of religious delusions in schizophrenia vary across countries: Lithuania 63% (n=295), England 24%, Germany/Austria 21%, Turkey 17%, Japan 7–11% — [Review in Revista de Psiquiatria Clínica (SciELO)](https://www.scielo.br/j/rpc/a/WQtchtzDZGPMs4p9mdz3PWb/?lang=en); [Psychiatria Danubina 2012](https://www.psychiatria-danubina.com/UserDocsImages/pdf/dnb_vol24%20Suppl%201_no/dnb_vol24%20Suppl%201_no_S65.pdf)
- In paranoid schizophrenia, the themes "God", "Christ", "Mary", "Satan/devil" and "hell" are prominent in religious hallucinations — [ResearchGate: Religious content of hallucinations in paranoid schizophrenia](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/231455996_Religious_content_of_hallucinations_in_paranoid_schizophrenia)

### Inferences
- If around 21–24% of schizophrenia inpatients in Western Europe have religious delusions, and schizophrenia affects roughly 0.3–0.7% of people (background figure, not sourced here), then in any large country thousands of people at any one time have religious delusions. Some of them believe they are Jesus or God. The few hundred notable "messiah claimants" in the Wikipedia lists are only the tip of the iceberg: people who found followers.
- What separates a clinical "Christ delusion" from a historically significant messiah claimant is mostly social: whether followers form a movement. This connects the psychiatric material to the historical lists.

### Gaps
- The often-quoted abstract figures of Bar-El et al. 2000 could NOT be confirmed against the full text in this session: **1,200 tourists** with severe Jerusalem-related mental problems referred over 13 years (1980–1993), of whom **470 were admitted** to hospital (Kfar Shaul Mental Health Centre), and 42 "pure" type III cases. These figures are widely cited and match background knowledge, but should be checked via DOI 10.1192/bjp.176.1.86. They correspond to around 90 referrals and 35–40 admissions per year.
- No reliable figure found for what share of patients with religious delusions specifically believe they are Jesus/Christ or God.
- No current (2020s) annual case numbers for Jerusalem syndrome were found.

## 6. Cross-cutting: How do these stories resemble or differ from the Jesus story?

### Takeaway
The recurring "Jesus-like" elements are a herald or prophet, a band of disciples, claimed miracles or signs, a confrontation with state power, a violent death, and followers who reinterpret the failure (non-death, return, "transformation"). The decisive difference is that almost no claimant's movement turned the leader's death into a lasting resurrection faith. The exceptions are partial survivals such as the Dönme, Ahmadiyya, Chabad messianists and the Unification Church.

### Cited Findings
- Execution or violent death at the hands of the authorities: Theudas (executed by the procurator) — [Vridar](https://vridar.org/2016/07/29/questioning-carrier-and-the-myth-that-the-jews-expected-a-messiah-3/); Bar Kokhba (revolt crushed at Bethar 135) — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/event/Bar-Kokhba-Revolt); John Nichols Thom (killed by soldiers 1838) — [The Fuel Project](https://thefuelproject.org/blog/2018/3/2/list-of-people-who-claimed-to-be-jesus)
- Followers reinterpret the "failure": Sabbatai Zevi's conversion did not shake many followers — [Commentary/Scholem](https://www.commentary.org/articles/gershom-scholem/the-holiness-of-sin/); some Chabad followers believe Schneerson never died — [Wikipedia: List of messiah claimants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_messiah_claimants); Creciendo en Gracia gave its dead leader the title "Melchizedek" after the promised transformation did not happen — [Wikipedia: José Luis de Jesús](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jos%C3%A9_Luis_de_Jes%C3%BAs)
- Taking over the Jesus story directly: Hong Xiuquan as "younger brother of Jesus" — [Britannica](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Hong-Xiuquan); Alan John Miller as reincarnated Jesus with a reincarnated "Mary Magdalene" partner — [Wikipedia: Divine Truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Truth); Inri Cristo, whose name echoes the cross inscription INRI — [Wikipedia: Inri Cristo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inri_Cristo)
- Moses/Exodus miracles instead of Jesus miracles: Theudas (parting the Jordan), the Egyptian (falling walls), Moses of Crete (parting the sea) — [A. Jacobs handout](https://andrewjacobs.org/ancientjudaism/handout4c.pdf); [Forward](https://forward.com/culture/346956/six-failed-messiahs-from-jewish-history/)
- Death instead of the promised salvation, in apocalyptic sects: Shakahola (death framed as a "triumphant passage") — [CBS News](https://www.cbsnews.com/news/doomsday-cult-leader-sentenced-murders-kenya-paul-mackenzie/); Jonestown 918 dead — [Wikipedia: Jonestown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jonestown)

### Inferences
- Rough typology for the German report: (1) political-royal messiahs (Antiquity: Athronges, Bar Kokhba; Sudan: Muhammad Ahmad; China: Hong Xiuquan), (2) sign prophets (Theudas, the Egyptian, Moses of Crete), (3) mystical-messianic movements (Abulafia, Sabbatai Zevi, Frank), (4) "reincarnated Jesus" figures of modern new religious movements (Vissarion, Inri Cristo, AJ Miller, de Jesús Miranda), (5) clinical cases (Jerusalem syndrome, the Three Christs).
- The Jewish claimants were mostly measured by whether they brought political liberation. Christian and modern claimants are mostly measured by the promised return or transformation. Both kinds of failure regularly led to reinterpretation instead of dissolution, which is the same dynamic sociologists describe for early Christianity (cognitive dissonance; Festinger's *When Prophecy Fails* would be the classic reference, not researched here).

### Gaps
- No academic synthesis was found that counts messiah claimants across all religions and eras. Any total number ("hundreds" or "thousands") is an estimate, and no source was found for it.
